The agents seemed to interact correctly with one another, and the sequence of steps they took was very logical. For instance, the inventory agent correctly found zero size-S tees while the accounting agent correctly prevented a duplicate payment after the 501 invoice was paid. That being said, there was an issue with stock shortage that should have resulted in the ticket not being resolved (since the customer did not end up with the tee).

Ticket 102 - Rent Notice

The agents seemed to handle the numbers and money arithmetic pretty well. Facilities correctly identified the rent about and shop date while the accounting agent confirmed a difference of $160 (so there wasn’t a negative balance as specified). Again, the agents’ steps were logical, and they delegated to one another as needed.

Ticket 103 - Bulk Hoodies

This, in my opinion, was the smoothest ticket for the agents to resolve. They worked together well, with the inventory agent correctly detecting a shortage of 12 hoodies and calling on accounting to compute the cost of replacing the 12 hoodies. The accounting agent also made a good call to refuse the purchase to avoid a negative balance (since the replacement cost was $264 while we only had $160).

What would’ve been simpler with one agent + tools?

Ticket 102 could have been handled with just one agent equipped with lease, cash, and payment approval tools since the flow was simple and linear. Ticket 103 could maybe be handled by one agent with tools for accounting and inventory because the calculation here was simple and direct. I don’t think one agent would be plausible for Ticket 101, however, since it involves so many delegations and steps compared to the other two tickets. Its complexities are best handled by multiple specialized agents.

3 Problems Our Model Could Solve:

One new problem could be a case of a low-stock college event order, where an organization requests 30+ Yale caps for an event. Our inventory agent could check the blue hat quantity by size, the accounting agent could compare unit cost and list price, and the boss agent could make the final judgement call on whether a discount could be allowed/is feasible. The customer service agent could then draft a response to the organization letting them know whether we could or could not discount their order.

Another possible problem would be an overdue supplier invoice review. If a new ticket were to reference an open invoice, the accounting agent could use invoice and vendor data to compare the due date to the desk.date_today metric, check the cash amount, and prepare a human approval request.

Lastly, another problem that could arise is a shop lease reminder. The facilities agent can retrieve the lease, compare the due date with the desk date, and delegate to the accounting agent to check our cash. The approval flow could record the payment and reduce checking after getting human approval.

3 Problems Our Model Could Not Solve:

One problem we wouldn’t be able to solve is receiving and fulfilling a reprint shipment. For instance, Bulldog Print Co may confirm the delivery of new size-S tees. The app as it exists now can’t receive stock or update inventory. It’s also unable to reserve apparel items for certain customers. It would need tools like receive_inventory, reserve_inventory, and fulfill_customer_order.

Another issue that could come up is sending a real approved customer email, as the model is currently only equipped to make a draft email, not actually send it out. We would need to add tools for email integration, possibly message templates for the customer service agent to follow, a human-one approval step, and log of sent/status.

Lastly, an issue that could come up is reconciling revenue and sales payments. The current model only treats cash as outgoing and doesn’t record customer payment, refunds, sales, etc. We would need a payment processor tool, cash tools, and fields for orders, sales, and refunds to be able to track incoming cash.
